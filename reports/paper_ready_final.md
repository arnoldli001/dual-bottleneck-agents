## Section 4 — Evidence from 2400-Round Experiments

Invalid rounds — where the teacher fails to produce a solvable constraint set — are encoded as Δ_t = 1.0 under the *global* caliber and excluded under the *valid-only* caliber.

### 4.3 Generator-Side Bottleneck (C and D)

Once the population game is enabled (C and D), the difficulty controller pushes tasks toward L2/L3. The teacher, however, cannot reliably author bit-vector and array constraints: valid rates collapse from 89.0% (A) and 75.5% (B) to 14.5% (C) and 9.5% (D). Critically, the failures are *not* graded by difficulty — they cluster at the moment of transition, because once the level is raised the teacher keeps failing and the controller keeps the level high. We call this the **pseudo-difficulty trap**: the system believes it is solving harder problems while in fact it spends its budget on unparseable or unsolvable task proposals.

Importantly, the student is not the bottleneck. Among all 2400 rounds, every round that produced a valid task was solved correctly; all failures are attributable to task generation.

### 4.4 The Dual-Bottleneck Picture

Sections 4.2 and 4.3 identify two independent failure modes. The judge-side bottleneck appears whenever the evaluation signal is not formally grounded: the system's self-assessment drifts from the truth and the drift accumulates. The generator-side bottleneck appears whenever the difficulty controller outruns the teacher's ability to author well-formed instances. A system is only safe inside the intersection of the two safe regions: judge reliability τ ≥ τ_c **and** generator valid rate ≥ 0.5. Outside that intersection the system still produces logs, still reports success on the rounds it can complete, and still appears to run — which is precisely why the failure is easy to miss.

## Section 5 — The Analytical Relationship Between Judge Reliability and Evaluation Bias

### 5.1 Noise Injection Protocol

We model an unreliable judge by flipping its verdict with probability (1 − τ)/2, where τ ∈ [0, 1] is the judge reliability. The flip is applied symmetrically (SAT → UNSAT and UNSAT → SAT); error and unknown verdicts are never flipped, since they carry no truth value to invert. Each (τ, condition) cell uses a fixed noise seed so the experiment is bit-for-bit reproducible. The protocol is implemented in `argus/z3_anchor.py::verify_with_noise`.

A detail that matters for Section 5.3: in this configuration **two independent judgments** are noised per round — the teacher's verdict on the generated task, and the student's verdict on its own encoding. They are produced by separate Z3 calls and therefore flip independently.

### 5.2 τ-Gradient Experiment Setup

We sweep τ over eight values {0.55, 0.60, 0.65, 0.70, 0.75, 0.80, 0.90, 1.00} for conditions B and D, with 50 rounds per cell (800 rounds total) using the **real** DeepSeek V4 Flash API. All rounds are pinned to difficulty L1 so that the generator-side bottleneck is held constant and only the judge's reliability varies.

### 5.4 Derivation of the analytical formula

**Assumption 1 (judge noise).** Each Z3 verdict is independently flipped with probability

$$p = \frac{1-\tau}{2}$$

**Assumption 2 (two independent judgments per round).** A round involves two separate Z3 calls: one validating the task the teacher produced (which becomes `expected`), and one validating the answer the student submitted. They are separate processes and flip independently.

A round is scored correct exactly when the two verdicts **agree** — both unflipped, or both flipped:

$$P(\text{correct}) = (1-p)^2 + p^2$$

Substituting p = (1−τ)/2, so that 1−p = (1+τ)/2:

$$P(\text{correct}) = \left(\frac{1+\tau}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1-\tau}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{(1+\tau)^2 + (1-\tau)^2}{4} = \frac{2 + 2\tau^2}{4} = \frac{1+\tau^2}{2}$$

and therefore

$$\boxed{\;\Delta_t(\tau) = 1 - P(\text{correct}) = \frac{1-\tau^2}{2}\;}$$

**Where the τ² comes from.** It is the sum of the two agreement terms: both-correct contributes ((1+τ)/2)² and both-flipped contributes ((1−τ)/2)². The cross terms cancel, leaving (1+τ²)/2. The exponent 2 is therefore the number of independent judgments per round — not any property of the judge alone.

**Where the 1/2 comes from.** At τ = 0 the expression gives Δ_t = 1/2: two independent random verdicts agree half the time. This is the binary symmetry of SAT/UNSAT tasks, and it is why the curve is anchored at 0.5 rather than 1.0 on the left.

### 5.5 Fit validation

Across the eight swept τ values the measured per-cell means track the closed form with

- MAE = **0.0258**, RMSE = 0.0329 (condition B)
- MAE = **0.0520**, RMSE = 0.0660 (condition D)

**A discriminating test.** The mechanism in Assumption 2 is not the only story that yields the same Δ_t(τ). An alternative — that the judge judges correctly with probability τ *and* the system accepts that judgment with probability τ — predicts P(correct) = τ² instead of (1+τ²)/2. These two differ sharply, so the measured `correct_rate` separates them without any fitting:

| Model | P(correct) | MAE vs observed correct_rate |
|---|---|---|
| Two independent judgments (**this work**) | (1 + τ²)/2 | **0.0389** |
| Judge + acceptance | τ² | 0.2291 |

Over the 16 (τ, condition) cells the two-judgment model is closer in 13, tied in 2, and loses in 1. At τ = 0.80 / condition B it predicts 0.8200 against a measured 0.8200. The acceptance story is rejected by a factor of six in MAE.

**Inverting the model.** Solving Δ_t = (1 − τ²)/2 for τ gives the design rule

$$\tau_c = \sqrt{1 - 2\,\Delta_t^{\max}}$$

so a system that can tolerate an error floor of Δ_t^max requires a judge of at least that reliability:

| Δ_t budget | Required τ_c |
|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.949 |
| 0.10 | 0.894 |
| 0.15 | 0.837 |
| 0.20 | 0.775 |
| 0.25 | 0.707 |

This is the practically useful form of the result: it converts a tolerance on evaluation error into a minimum reliability requirement for the judge, without needing to re-run the sweep. Note the reachability bound — no judge reliability can hold Δ_t below 0.5, since Δ_t(0) = 1/2.

### 5.6 Theorem 1: Judge Reliability Threshold

**Assumptions.**

**(A1) Binary symmetric judge noise.** The judge returns a verdict in `{SAT, UNSAT}` and flips it with
probability $p = (1-\tau)/2$, symmetrically; `error` and `unknown` verdicts are never flipped, since they
carry no truth value to invert.

**(A2) Two independent judgments per round.** Every round involves two separate judgments — the
teacher-side judgment that validates the generated task and fixes its `expected` label, and the
student-side judgment that validates the submitted answer. They are separate Z3 calls and therefore flip
independently.

**(A3) Agreement scoring on a binary task.** A round is scored correct iff the two verdicts **agree**. On a
binary SAT/UNSAT task two independent random verdicts agree with probability exactly $1/2$, which fixes the
$\tau \to 0$ limit.

**Theorem 1.** *Under (A1)–(A3) the expected evaluation bias of the system is*

$$\Delta_t(\tau) \;=\; \frac{1-\tau^{2}}{2}$$

*with no free parameters; equivalently $P(\text{correct}) = (1+\tau^{2})/2$, and $\Delta_t(0) = 1/2$.*

**Proof sketch.** The three paths are exhaustive. *Both unflipped*, probability $\left(\frac{1+\tau}{2}\right)^{2}$:
the verdicts agree, the round is scored correct, error contribution **0**. *Exactly one flipped*,
probability $2p(1-p) = \frac{1-\tau^{2}}{2}$: the verdicts disagree, the round is scored incorrect, error
contribution **1**. *Both flipped*, probability $\left(\frac{1-\tau}{2}\right)^{2}$: the verdicts **still
agree with each other**, so the round is scored correct and the joint error is invisible to the system —
error contribution **0**. Only the middle path carries bias, so $\Delta_t$ is exactly its probability. The
$\tau^{2}$ comes from summing the two agreement terms, whose cross terms cancel, which is why the exponent
is the *number of independent judgments* rather than a property of the judge. Full derivation:
`reports/theorem1_proof_sketch.md`.

**Inverse (design) form.** Solving for $\tau$ gives the minimum admissible judge reliability at a tolerated
bias $\Delta_t^{\max}$:

$$\tau_c \;=\; \sqrt{1 - 2\,\Delta_t^{\max}}$$

Because $\Delta_t(0) = 1/2$, no judge reliability can hold the bias below $1/2$, so the rule is defined only
for $\Delta_t^{\max} \in [0, 1/2)$.

**Corollary (judge scarcity).** $\tau_c$ is a function of the tolerated bias, **not** a constant of the
system. At $\Delta_t^{\max} = 0.35$ a judge that errs on roughly one verdict in five ($\tau \approx 0.55$)
still keeps the system inside the safe region; tightening the tolerance to $0.05$ requires
$\tau \approx 0.95$. See `reports/table4d_theorem_implications.md` for the deployment rule, the runtime
calibration proposal, and the open generalisations to M-ary tasks and to $k$ judgment layers.

### 5.7 Relation to the Ratchet bound

Ratchet's analysis gives (1 − τ)/2 as the probability that a **single** judgment is flipped. Our quantity is the steady-state error of a **double**-judgment system. The two are related by the agreement probability, and the distinction is structural rather than numerical:

| Setting | Noisy events per decision | Error |
|---|---|---|
| Skill elimination (Ratchet) | one judgment, then act on it | (1 − τ)/2 |
| Answer evaluation (this work) | judge's verdict **and** system's verdict must agree | (1 − τ²)/2 |

The map between them is the probability that exactly one of the two judgments flips:

$$\Delta_t = 2p(1-p) \;\xrightarrow{p \to 0}\; 2p = 1-\tau$$

So the two agree to first order in the high-reliability limit, and diverge quadratically away from it. At τ = 0.95 the single-judgment bound predicts 0.025 while the double-judgment model predicts 0.049: requiring two independent verdicts to agree costs a factor of two in tolerated error at that reliability. Ratchet's bound is the special case of a system that acts on one judgment; the formula here generalises it to systems that act on agreement between two.

### 5.8 The Q4 hypothesis: refuted, then reconstructed

**Refutation.** The original hypothesis was that τ_c(D) > τ_c(B) because D's population game advances the difficulty into L2/L3. Under L1 isolation the real-API sweep returns **τ_c(D) = τ_c(B) = 0.55**, and an earlier mock-LLM gap of 0.90 vs 0.55 does not reproduce. Pinning the difficulty removes exactly the mechanism the hypothesis invoked; the mock gap was an artefact of deterministic task content collapsing Δ_t to a {0, 1} sequence. The hypothesis as stated is **not supported**.

**Reconstruction.** The intuition behind it survives, but the causal variable is different. A round succeeds only if a valid task exists *and* the student judges it correctly, so by the chain rule

$$1 - \Delta_t^{\text{global}} = (1 - \Delta_t^{\text{gen}})(1 - \Delta_t^{\text{judge}})$$

This holds to machine precision on the 2400-round data: [Table 4c: composition law — insert `tables/table4c_dual_bottleneck_law.md` here].

The two bottlenecks therefore **share one budget**. Given a tolerated global error Δ_t^max, the amount left for judge noise is

$$\Delta_{\text{judge}}^{\text{budget}} = 1 - \frac{1-\Delta_t^{\max}}{1-\Delta_t^{\text{gen}}}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tau_c = \sqrt{1 - 2\,\Delta_{\text{judge}}^{\text{budget}}}$$

τ_c is thus not an intrinsic property of a condition's mechanisms — it is **derived** from how much of the budget the generator bottleneck leaves over. A condition with a large Δ_t_gen needs a more reliable judge for the same overall tolerance, but the driver is the budget split, not τ_c itself.

Concretely, at Δ_t^max = 0.60: condition B (Δ_t_gen = 0.245) requires τ_c ≈ 0.24, while C and D (Δ_t_gen = 0.855 and 0.905) cannot meet the budget at **any** judge reliability, because their generator bottleneck alone already exceeds it. That is the correct form of the Q4 intuition, and it turns the dual-bottleneck framework of Section 4.4 from a diagnosis into an accounting rule.

## Section 6 — Extending Generator Capability

### 6.1 The Four-Layer Constraint Generation Framework

Section 4.3 showed that free-form code generation is the binding constraint on the generator side. We therefore replace it with a four-layer pipeline:

1. **JSON skeleton generation.** The teacher prompt asks for a JSON object (`variables`, `constraints`, `expected_sat`) instead of Python code, removing the bracket-matching and variable-declaration failure classes outright.
2. **Deterministic rendering.** `render_z3_code` turns the skeleton into Z3 Python code by template substitution. Because the renderer never guesses, the output is syntactically valid by construction; malformed skeletons raise a typed `TaskJSONInvalid` rather than failing silently.
3. **Symbolic feedback.** Z3 error codes are mapped to symbolic constraints (for example, a timeout appends "reduce the constraint count to at most five") which are injected into the next attempt's prompt.
4. **Curriculum controller.** Sub-level variable counts within each difficulty level are raised after five consecutive rounds with valid rate > 0.80 and lowered after three consecutive rounds below 0.50, damping the oscillation that produced the pseudo-difficulty trap.

### 6.2 Before/After Comparison (Real API)

We validate the constraint framework on 240 rounds (4 conditions × 3 levels × 20 rounds) using real DeepSeek V4 Flash API. This is a capability probe rather than a full replication of the 2400-round baseline: the legacy comparison uses the existing 2400-round logs, while the framework is measured on a deliberately smaller budget because each level is requested explicitly rather than left to the difficulty controller.

With the framework in place, valid rates under the real API are L1 = 98.8% (79/80), L2 = 71.2% (57/80), L3 = 58.8% (47/80). The legacy teacher, by contrast, produced only a handful of L2/L3 valid tasks across 2400 rounds (11 and 114 respectively), because most attempts failed before a task could be verified at all. Because the two runs use different round budgets and different denominators, Section 6.2 compares **capability**, not rates; `reports/improvement_validation_real.md` §2.1 documents the caliber difference.

### 6.3 A valve defect the mock could not find

The first real-API run of the framework scored 100% on L1 but only 10% on L2. Every failure carried the same diagnostic:

```
schema_invalid: constraint '(a & b) == 0x0F' references undeclared identifier 'x0F'
```

The validator tokenised constraints with a bare identifier regex, which splits the hexadecimal literal `0x0F` into `0` and `x0F`; the fragment was then rejected as an undeclared variable. L1 (integer arithmetic) rarely uses hex masks, so it was unaffected; L2 is bit-vector theory, where `0xFF`-style masks are the standard idiom, so the validator rejected essentially every well-formed task. The failure was in our own guardrail, not in the model.

The fix substitutes numeric literals (hex, binary, octal, decimal, underscore-separated, scientific) with placeholders before scanning, and extends the Z3 builtin whitelist (`BitVecVal`, `BV2Int`, `LShR`, …). L2 rose from 10% to 71.2% and L3 reached 58.8%. Seven regression tests pin the behaviour.

We record this because it is a methodological point rather than an implementation detail: a mock LLM emits hand-written, well-formed payloads and will never exercise the validator against the idioms a real model actually uses. The bug was invisible for the entire mock-based validation and appeared within twenty rounds of using the real API.

### 6.4 Validity Rates Under the Real API

After the fix, the remaining invalid rounds are all `error_code=1: LLM did not return valid JSON` — the model produced output that could not be parsed at all. That is a genuine capability boundary rather than a framework defect, and it is the honest floor for this approach: the framework guarantees that a well-formed skeleton becomes a well-formed constraint set, but it cannot guarantee that the model emits a well-formed skeleton.

### 6.5 Statistical Tests

Because the legacy per-level denominators exclude its failed rounds, only the L1 comparison is caliber-consistent. There the improved teacher shows a lower Δ_t (0.013 vs 0.082; Welch t = 5.43, p = 6.0e-8, Cohen's d = 0.89, large).
