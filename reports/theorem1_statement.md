# Theorem 1: Judge Reliability Threshold

> **Role.** Formal statement of the result derived informally in §5.4 and fitted in §5.5 of

## Assumptions

The theorem is stated for a self-evolving system that evaluates itself once per round. Three assumptions
are required, and all three are load-bearing.

**(A1) Binary symmetric judge noise.**
The judge — a Z3 formal anchor — returns a verdict in `{SAT, UNSAT}`. With probability

$$p = \frac{1-\tau}{2}$$

it returns the **flipped** verdict, symmetrically (`SAT → UNSAT` and `UNSAT → SAT`). Verdicts of type
`error` or `unknown` are **never** flipped, because they carry no truth value to invert. Here
$\tau \in [0,1]$ is the **judge reliability**: $\tau = 1$ is a perfect judge, $\tau = 0$ is an adversarial
one. Every judgment draws its own independent noise.

*(A1) is the definition of τ used throughout the paper and is implemented in
`argus/z3_anchor.py::verify_with_noise`.*

**(A2) Two independent judgments per round.**
Each round involves exactly two separate judgments:

- the **teacher-side** judgment, which validates the task the teacher produced and thereby fixes its
  `expected` label;
- the **student-side** judgment, which validates the answer the student submitted.

They are executed as separate Z3 calls, so their noise draws are independent.

**(A3) Agreement scoring on a binary task.**
A round is scored **correct** if and only if the two verdicts **agree**. On a binary SAT/UNSAT task, two
independent random verdicts agree with probability exactly $1/2$, which fixes the $\tau \to 0$ limit.

> **Why (A3) matters.** It is the assumption that carries the factor $1/2$ in the final formula. It is a
> property of the *task's arity*, not of the judge. Relaxing it to M-ary tasks is Open Problem 1 in
> `table4d_theorem_implications.md`.

---

## Statement

**Theorem 1 (Judge Reliability Threshold).** *Under (A1)–(A3), the expected evaluation bias of the system
is*

$$\boxed{\;\Delta_t(\tau) \;=\; \frac{1-\tau^{2}}{2}\;}$$

*with no free parameters.*

Equivalently, the round-level success probability is

$$P(\text{correct}) \;=\; 1 - \Delta_t(\tau) \;=\; \frac{1+\tau^{2}}{2}$$

**Inverse (design) form.** Solving for τ gives the minimum judge reliability required to hold the
evaluation bias at or below a tolerated level $\Delta_t^{\max}$:

$$\boxed{\;\tau_c \;=\; \sqrt{\,1 - 2\,\Delta_t^{\max}\,}\;}$$

**Reachability bound.** Because $\Delta_t(0) = 1/2$, no judge reliability can hold the evaluation bias
below $1/2$. The inverse form is therefore defined only for $\Delta_t^{\max} \in [0,\,1/2)$.

---

## Corollary (Judge Scarcity)

$\tau_c$ is a function of the tolerated bias, **not** a constant of the system:

| $\Delta_t^{\max}$ | 0.05 | 0.10 | 0.15 | 0.20 | 0.25 | 0.35 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| required $\tau_c$ | 0.949 | 0.894 | 0.837 | 0.775 | 0.707 | **0.548** |

Two consequences, and they pull in opposite directions:

1. **A mediocre judge suffices for a tolerant system.** At $\Delta_t^{\max} = 0.35$, a judge that gets
   roughly one verdict in five wrong ($\tau \approx 0.55$) still keeps the system inside the safe region.
   "Judge scarcity" is therefore not automatically fatal.
2. **Tightening the tolerance is expensive.** Moving from $\Delta_t^{\max} = 0.35$ to $0.05$ raises the
   requirement from $\tau \approx 0.55$ to $\tau \approx 0.95$ — from "an off-the-shelf formal checker"
   to "essentially no noise at all".

## Relation to Ratchet

Ratchet (2026) analyses a **single**-judgment setting: one verdict is produced and then acted upon, so
the error is the flip probability itself,

$$\Delta_t^{\text{Ratchet}} \;=\; p \;=\; \frac{1-\tau}{2}$$

Theorem 1 analyses a **double**-judgment setting: the round is scored by *agreement*, so a single flip is
no longer sufficient to hide the error. Writing $p = (1-\tau)/2$,

$$\Delta_t^{\text{Theorem 1}} \;=\; 2p(1-p) \;=\; \frac{1-\tau^{2}}{2}$$

The map between the two is exactly the probability that **one of the two judgments flips and the other
does not**. Hence

$$\Delta_t^{\text{Theorem 1}} \;=\; 2p(1-p) \;\xrightarrow{\;p \to 0\;}\; 2p \;=\; 1-\tau$$

so the two bounds agree to **first order** in the high-reliability limit and diverge **quadratically**
away from it.

> Ratchet's bound is the special case of a system that acts on **one** judgment. Theorem 1 generalises it
> to systems that act on **agreement between two**. A numerical comparison at τ = 0.95 is given in §5.7.

---

## Remark 1 (Rejected alternative model)

The mechanism in (A2) is not the only story that produces a $\tau^2$-shaped curve, and the two stories are
empirically separable. The competing model holds that *the judge judges correctly with probability τ
**and** the system accepts that judgment with probability τ*:

$$P(\text{correct}) = \tau \cdot \tau = \tau^{2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta_t = 1 - \tau^{2}$$

**This model is rejected by the data without any fitting.** The §5.5 discriminating test compares both
against the measured `correct_rate`:

| Model | $P(\text{correct})$ | MAE vs observed `correct_rate` |
|---|---|---|
| Two independent judgments — **Theorem 1** | $(1+\tau^{2})/2$ | **0.0389** |
| Judge + acceptance — rejected | $\tau^{2}$ | 0.2291 |

Over the 16 $(τ, \text{condition})$ cells the Theorem 1 model is closer in **13**, tied in **2** and loses
in **1**. At τ = 0.80 / condition B it predicts 0.8200 against a measured 0.8200. The acceptance story is
worse by a factor of **six** in MAE.

The separation is equally sharp on the bias scale. At $\tau = 0.55$ the rejected model predicts

$$\Delta_t = 1 - 0.55^{2} = 0.6975$$

against a measured 0.3000 (B) / 0.3200 (D) — off by more than a factor of two. **No result in this paper
uses $\Delta_t = 1-\tau^2$.**

## Empirical τ_c

Under the L1-isolated real-API sweep, **no** τ value triggers the within-τ degeneration criterion
(τ-ratio, minimum absolute increase, and the 1.5·SE guard all fail to fire). The detected value is
therefore

$$\tau_c(B) \;=\; \tau_c(D) \;=\; 0.55$$

This is the **lower edge of the swept grid**, not a detected transition. It must be read as an **upper
bound** on $\tau_c$, not as the system's true tolerance — the closed form above replaces it with a
quantity that does not need to be measured.

Inverting at that value gives the corresponding tolerance

$$\Delta_t^{\max} \;=\; \frac{1 - 0.55^{2}}{2} \;=\; 0.34875 \;\approx\; \mathbf{0.349}$$

which is consistent with the measured per-cell bias at that τ (0.300 B / 0.320 D) to within the fit's MAE.
